## Method, and why these tools

144 URLs from `throxy.com/sitemap.xml`, reduced to 72 by dropping Framer's
cross-product duplicate case-study routes and sampling 3 of 70 programmatic-SEO
glossary pages. 71 fetched, 465,902 characters of page text.

| Tool | Version | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `requests` | 2.34.2 | Fetch. Same choice `discovery.py` makes. Synchronous is correct at one request per second — concurrency would only help if politeness were not the binding constraint. |
| `scrapling.parser.Selector` | 0.4.11 | Parse. Already a project dependency and already the parser `discovery.py` uses, so the research path and the product path cannot drift. Its stealth *fetchers* are deliberately not used, exactly as `discovery.py` documents. |
| `lxml` | 6.1.1 | Under Scrapling. C-speed tree building; nothing hand-rolled. |
| `urllib.robotparser` | stdlib | `robots.txt` honoured before every fetch. |
| **Not** `crawl4ai` | — | Not installed here, and it exists to drive a headless browser. Nothing on this target needs one. |
| **Not** Playwright / Chromium | — | See below. |

**A browser was deliberately not used, and the reason is the interesting part.**
A probe showed 693KB of HTML carrying 15.6KB of visible text — a 2.2% ratio that
looks exactly like a client-rendered JavaScript app. It is not. Framer
static-exports the copy into the served markup; the bulk is inline runtime and
styling. Rendering with Chromium would have cost roughly 1.5s and ~300MB per
page to produce text already in hand.

The general rule this illustrates: **probe the target's rendering first, then
pick the library.** A low text-to-HTML ratio is evidence, not proof, that a
browser is needed. Choosing a headless browser by default is the most common way
scraping work becomes slow, fragile and expensive for no gain.

Infrastructure was determined by DNS resolution of five subdomains. No login, no
paid data, nothing behind a wall.

## Verdict first

**Read this one differently from the other four.** Floqer, Strawberry, Crux and
Outmate are software companies selling software. Throxy is a services company
with proprietary tooling inside it, and says so: their About page describes
"flipping Software-as-a-Service into Service-as-a-Software."

Three facts carry the teardown.

1. They built a **4-billion-record contact database from public company
   registries and government data** — Companies House alone holds 6 million UK
   businesses with downloadable director records — because their buyers in
   manufacturing and logistics are not on LinkedIn and not in Apollo.
2. A co-founder of an AI sales company told VentureBeat, on the record: *"We
   barely use AI there. We think the best messages, at least as in today, are
   generated by humans."* AI does data processing and qualification. Humans write.
3. **$1.5M ARR on a reported three employees** — but see the correction below.
   The figure does not survive an arithmetic check once you know they have made
   3.6 million dials.

**Correction, added 2026-09-17.** An earlier version of this file called the
three-employee figure "the best unit economics in the series by a distance".
That was repeating a number without testing it. Throxy's own blog now states
**3.6 million cold calls** made. At an aggressive 250 dials per SDR per day that
is roughly 14,400 SDR-days, or about 24 SDR-years of work — across an operating
life of about 2.5 years, a minimum of ten to twenty full-time dialers. Their
posts refer to "our SDR team", interviews with multiple SDRs, and an internal
dialer rollout "across our SDR team".

So "three employees" must mean three core or HQ staff, with the calling function
staffed by contractors or a separate pool, or the figure predates the calling
operation entirely. The revenue-per-head story is still good; it is not the
outlier it first appeared to be, and the original framing was too generous.

**Fresh, and unreported elsewhere:** on 13 September 2026 Throxy published a
short notice that co-founder **Bergen Merey** is leaving. The About page already
lists only two founders. That notice credits Bergen with getting Throxy into Y
Combinator. A co-founder departure three months after a $6.2M seed is material.

## The data moat

> "Every single country has company registers. Every single company also has
> directors, and all that information is publicly available and you can download
> it. Yet at my previous companies, we were not using that."
> — Pablo Jiménez de Parga Ramos

LinkedIn has 1.1bn profiles. Throxy claims 4bn records from registries and
government sources. This is not a bigger Apollo — it is a **different
population**. Apollo, ZoomInfo and Clay index the digitally visible. A 40-person
injection-moulding firm in Bavaria with a two-page site and an owner who has
never posted on LinkedIn is invisible to all of them, and fully documented in a
national register with the director named.

### The second-order insight, which is the better one

Data decay is the tax on every B2B database — ~30–40% of records go stale
annually, which is why waterfall enrichment exists. Throxy noticed the tax is
not uniform:

> "In traditional spaces, people stay — if you find information about a person
> in 2011, they're probably still there."

They picked the one segment where a contact database **appreciates instead of
decaying**. That is the single most valuable piece of strategic reasoning in the
whole series.

**Where to be sceptical:** "4 billion records" counts registry rows, not
reachable decision-makers. A register gives a legal entity, a registered address
and named directors — not an email, not a mobile, often not a role. Turning a
registry row into a contactable human is the actual product and none of it is
described publicly. They never claim otherwise.

## The bet nobody else made

> "AI-generated messaging does not resonate a lot with the market, and it's not
> very effective. We barely use AI there."

A YC company, Base10-backed, selling AI-powered outbound, whose co-founder says
they barely use AI for messaging. Their own blog post is titled *"AI SDRs Are
Killing Sales"* and names 11x and Artisan as recipients of hype capital.

The argument is not anti-AI. It is a claim about *where in the pipeline* AI
earns its keep: data processing and qualification, where there is a right answer
and a feedback signal — not message writing, where the failure mode is subtle,
unmeasurable and expensive.

**off_CRM already agrees and has never said so.** `ai/bandit.py` allocates
traffic between owner-approved variants and never decides whether a rewrite goes
live. A human writes; the machine optimises distribution. Three of five
companies in this series have now independently landed on
human-writes-machine-assists. Treat it as the consensus of people who run
outbound at volume, not as a limitation to apologise for.

### Kairos

Launched weeks ago. ~100,000 cold emails per week, two and a half years of
campaign history, paired with transcripts from thousands of cold calls, all
feeding one system so that every reply, transcript and booked meeting sharpens
the next campaign. Named for the Greek god of the fleeting moment, as against
Chronos — the claim is that timing, not volume, is the variable worth
optimising.

**This is the loop `COPY_OR_REFUSE.md` Add 1 describes.** Throxy has it running
on real volume with a second data source nobody else has: **call transcripts**.
An email tells you someone replied; a recording tells you what they said and
why. Pairing the two is the strongest technical idea in this teardown.

## The operating model: who actually calls, and who actually writes

Added 2026-09-17, because "we barely use AI" raises the obvious question of what
they do instead. All of this is from their own published posts.

### Who calls whom

**Throxy employs its own human SDR team, and those SDRs phone the prospect
directly, introducing themselves with the client's company name.** It is a
white-label outsourced calling floor. The prospect does not hear "Throxy" — they
hear the client's name. From the AI-receptionist post: *"Our SDRs now say the
client's company name clearly in the first three seconds of every call."*

Volume, by their own numbers: **3.6 million cold calls**, **355 weekly client
reports**, **2,764 persona breakdowns**. The weekly per-client report is the
service artifact — the thing the customer actually receives.

### They build plenty of AI — just not for the words

They built their own dialer, **Maestro**, described as AI-native rather than a
legacy parallel dialer. Three functions, in their description:

- **Predictive answer analysis** — predict who will actually pick up, so dialing
  effort goes at conversations that will happen.
- **Optimal SDR-to-lead matching** — route a connected call to the best-suited
  rep, not to whoever is next in the queue.
- **Sale-type-aware preparation** — brief the rep for the specific pitch before
  the call connects.

Claimed result: **+121% meetings booked per week three weeks after rollout**, and
3–4× more meetings per hour of dialing time. Both self-reported.

So the split is precise, and "we barely use AI" was about messaging
specifically: **AI does routing, prediction, preparation, data processing and
qualification. Humans do the conversation and the copy.**

### Their most original finding

From analysing those 355 weekly reports: **an AI receptionist is the single most
common outcome they log on a dial** — appearing more than twice as often as a
human saying "not interested" — and in 44% of weekly reports it is the number
one outcome across the whole account. Senior marketing titles are worst; calls
to marketing leaders route through AI almost by default.

Their conclusion is genuinely novel: every famous cold-call opener (Josh Braun's
permission opener, Belal Batrawy's mic drop, Gong's 300-million-call ranking) is
engineered for the half-second in which a *human* decides whether to keep
listening. A voice agent has nothing to disarm. So they now write the first line
**for a routing model to parse** — company name, clearly, inside three seconds —
and save the human opener for when a person actually answers.

*"Writing an opener a machine has to parse is a completely different job to
writing one a person judges. Almost nobody in outbound is doing it deliberately
yet."*

### Who writes the emails, and are there templates

**Throxy's own writers write the copy, and Throxy sends it from infrastructure
Throxy owns.** Clients are not handed templates to use themselves — that is the
whole point of a done-for-you service. Their messaging page is explicit that
templates are the thing they are against: *"Every message starts with research.
Not templates."*

The stated method, four steps, in their words:

1. **Writers build the base message** — a handcrafted framework per campaign,
   written by someone who knows the ICP and sales motion.
2. **AI enriches with dynamic context** — injecting verified facts like team
   size, new hires or funding. *"It supports the message, not replaces it."*
3. **Playbooks guide structure** — proven frameworks mapped to the recipient's
   role and industry.
4. **Every message is reviewed by a human** — *"No automation giveaways."*

They name what they consider the four failure modes: fully AI-generated copy,
shallow personalisation ("saw you went to Stanford"), one message for every
role, and no hook logic or segmentation at all.

### The scripts, verbatim

There are no email templates published. There *is* a published call script —
they gave the whole thing away in a blog post, which is itself a content
strategy. Reproduced here because you asked whether it could be found.

**Opener:**

> "Hi, this is [name]. I understand I'm calling you completely out of the blue.
> If it's a bad time, just tell me to bugger off."

Their reasoning: nobody says "bugger off", so it lands as a surprise, gets a
laugh, and hands the prospect an easy exit — which is why they stay. That buys
about 30 seconds.

**The short pitch** — three things only: who you are, what the company does in
one plain sentence, and one question:

> "We help [company type] book more qualified meetings using a mix of AI and
> human cold calling. Quick question, how are you generating leads at the
> moment?"

**Pain-point drilling** — find one pain, go deep before moving on. Their stated
questions:

> "How are you doing lead generation at the moment?" · "What tools are you
> using, and how are you finding them?" · "Are you getting enough data out of
> them?" · "How big is your total addressable market?" · "Which industries are
> you selling into?"

**The ask**, at four to five minutes, after two pains surfaced and addressed:

> "Look, I don't want to take up too much of your time. Can we set something up
> next week to go into this in more depth?"

And the line that explains their whole thesis:

> "There's no formula for this. No AI can tell you the exact second. It requires
> reading the energy of the call. … This is why throxy runs a human SDR team."

## The evidence, and it is the best in the series

Four case studies, every one with a named customer, a named person, a job title
and specific figures.

| Customer | Sector | Numbers | Named |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Imnoo** | Manufacturing (CNC quoting), email-first | 3–5 meetings/week · 90% deliverability lift · 35+ hrs saved/wk | Leon Redegeld (BDR); Niklas Gerlach (COO) in the funding release |
| **Sybilion** | Industrial procurement, phone-first | **84K+ dials** · **97% booked via phone** · 78.7% qualified | Bjol R. Frenkenberger (CEO). Closed a trial with Grupo Fertiberia. |
| **exactly.ai** | Creative AI, phone-first | **81K+ dials** · **83.3% booked via phone** · 82.9% qualified | Tonia Samsonova (CEO), chosen over 10 vendors |
| **Fuse** | Health tech, both channels | 2× ROI month 1 · 0 SDRs hired · $10k+ ACV deal in month 1 | Sho Sugihara (Founder) |

### The number that should bother off_CRM most

**165,000 dials across two flagship accounts. 97% and 83% of meetings booked by
telephone.**

For buyers in industrial procurement the phone booked almost everything and
email booked almost nothing. off_CRM has no calling capability, no dialer, no
transcription, no call logging — not "unbuilt", not on the board at all. If the
segment where GTM data is genuinely scarce is also the segment that converts on
the phone, then an email-first architecture is aimed at the segment where
competition is fiercest and data is cheapest.

### Transparency runs opposite to Outmate

**Throxy names its company and hides its vendors** — THROXY LTD, UK address, VAT
number, named governing law, but a boilerplate privacy policy with no
subprocessor list, no model provider, no hosting region, no retention schedule.

**Outmate names its vendors and hides its company** — eleven subprocessors listed
in detail, and "governed by the laws of our jurisdiction of incorporation",
never stated.

Neither is complete. Throxy's gap is the easier to fix and the less alarming:
you can ask a company with a Companies House number what it runs on. You cannot
sue a company that will not say where it is incorporated.

*Note:* 128 City Road, London EC1V 2NX is a widely used registered-office
service, not evidence of a London office. Normal at this stage; recorded only so
the detail is not over-read.

| Deliverability | Multi-domain, warm-up, monitoring, per-client infra | SPF/DKIM/DMARC preflight, SES lanes, durable jobs, signed unsubscribe, feedback auto-pause | **Rough parity, and off_CRM is more automated.** Its most underrated module. |

| Closed loop | Kairos — email + call transcripts, 100k sends/wk | `TemplateScore`, `bandit.py`, `dist_metrics` — built, email only | Same architecture, no volume, no second channel. |
| Business model | Retainer + per attended meeting | None | Outcome pricing is the model a one-person shop can actually sell. |

**The fair comparison.** Throxy is not a near-term competitor. They sell a
managed service to industrial firms in Europe; off_CRM is an unreleased codebase
with one user. Reading them as a rival is the same category error as reading
Strawberry as one.

They matter for a different reason: **the technical core is small enough for one
person to hold.** A registry-derived database, a sending stack, a dialer, and a
feedback loop. The part that is not small is the SDR floor — and that is people,
not code. Of the five companies, this is the one whose *engineering* is most
clearly within reach of one developer, which is exactly why the services half
of it is the part off_CRM cannot copy.

## Verdict

1. **Their central insight is free.** Pick the segment where the data everyone
   else rents is worthless, then get data elsewhere. Registries are free,
   downloadable and legal. The insight is not the scraping — it is realising
   that LinkedIn-indexed contacts are the most competed-for and least
   defensible input in outbound.
2. **You have the scraper; you do not have the strategy.** `discovery.py`
   already does guarded public-web discovery with Scrapling, Crawl4AI,
   robots.txt compliance, SSRF guards and DNS checks — the hard, careful half of
   what Throxy built. It currently points at the open web generically rather
   than at a structured, high-value corpus. Pointing it at a national company
   register is a parser and a configuration decision, not a new system. Highest
   leverage repurposing of existing code in the series.
3. **Data that appreciates beats data that decays.** If off_CRM ever picks a
   vertical, pick on this axis rather than market size. It compounds; market
   size does not.
4. **The phone gap is real — decide about it deliberately.** 165,000 dials,
   83–97% of meetings by phone. Either accept off_CRM is an email-and-content
   system for digitally-native buyers and say so, or acknowledge the segment
   with the best data moat is the one that answers the telephone. It is
   currently undecided, which is the worst option.
5. **Outcome pricing is the only model a single person can sell.** Retainer plus
   per attended meeting. No seats, no credits. A buyer cannot lose, which
   collapses the trust problem a one-person vendor with no logos cannot
   otherwise solve. Five briefs have circled who buys off_CRM; this is the first
   pricing structure that would work for a seller with no reputation.
6. **The personal one.** The engineering underneath Throxy is a registry
   database, a sending stack, a dialer and a feedback loop — all of which exist
   in off_CRM in some form, except the dialer. The difference is not capability.
   Throxy sold a service before they had a platform and let the platform grow
   out of the service; off_CRM built the platform first and has never sold
   anything. That is the whole gap, for the fifth time.
7. **What they actually automated is the instructive part.** They built AI for
   routing, answer prediction and call preparation — the parts with a
   measurable right answer — and kept humans on the copy and the conversation,
   where failure is subtle and unmeasurable. That is a better rule for deciding
   what to automate than any capability list, and off_CRM's `bandit.py` already
   obeys it.

## Confidence

**High** on corporate identity (stated in their own Terms), the funding round
and investor list, the founder quotes (VentureBeat interview, republished by
them), and the infrastructure (DNS records). **High** on the Bergen Merey
departure — they published it, dated 13 September 2026.

**Low, and revised down** on $1.5M ARR at three employees: reported by
VentureBeat from a founder interview, not audited, and it fails the arithmetic
check against 3.6 million dials (see the correction near the top). Treat the ARR
as plausible and the headcount as referring to core staff only.

**Note on totals.** The About page says 2m+ cold calls; a later blog post says
3.6 million. Both are theirs, published weeks apart. The site is inconsistent
because it is not maintained in one pass — the same disease the Outmate teardown
records, in a milder form, and worth remembering before quoting either figure.

**Vendor-reported and unverified**: every case-study metric, and the cumulative
100+ clients / 2m+ calls / 10m+ emails / $100m+ pipeline figures — though these
are materially better evidenced than anything in the previous four teardowns,
since customers, people and job titles are all named.

**Treat with care**: "4bn+ contacts" counts registry records, not reachable
decision-makers.
